FFP-201 secures what an operator carries and transmits. There is a category of threat it does not address: what can be placed in the spaces they occupy, hidden in the equipment they use, or used to physically track them in the environment.

That is the gap FFP-401 is designed to close.

## What Changes Between FFP-201 and FFP-401

FFP-201 is about controlling what leaves a person. Device hardening, credential security, communications discipline, and data-broker footprint reduction are all about limiting what an adversary can collect from what the operator does and what their devices broadcast.

FFP-401 is about detecting what is being done to a person. Technical surveillance countermeasures address devices planted in spaces. Direction-finding tradecraft addresses RF-emitting trackers and surveillance hardware. Non-destructive entry skills address understanding how access to spaces is gained and secured. These are active detection and clearing skills, not just protective configurations.

The prerequisite structure reflects this. FFP-401 requires FFP-201 plus a Linux/CLI proficiency bar, because students spend significant time operating SDR and direction-finding tools from a terminal. The course assumes that device hygiene and communications discipline are already operational habits, not concepts being introduced for the first time.

## The Build-vs-Buy Approach

FFP-401's most distinctive curriculum element is its direction-finding instruction: students are issued a commercial handheld RF and Wi-Fi detection unit as a buy-side reference, then build their own equivalent unit from open-source firmware and raw hardware during the course.

The reason for this approach is the same reason that [FFF-401](/courses/fff-401/) has students build their platform rather than receiving a pre-built one: an operator who built the tool understands how it works, why it detects what it detects, and what its limitations are. An operator who received a finished tool and learned to operate it does not.

Direction-finding tools detect RF-emitting devices based on signal characteristics, frequency, timing, power level, modulation. An operator who built one from components understands those characteristics in a way that makes them a better interpreter of what the tool finds. They can reason about why a detection might be a false positive, why a specific frequency is suspicious in a given context, and how to position and use the equipment to improve result quality.

The same logic runs through the TSCM instruction. Knowing how to sweep a space is more reliable when you understand how surveillance hardware is placed, powered, and accessed, which is why FFP-401 includes hardware teardown, non-destructive entry fundamentals, and space-clearing instruction alongside the detection methods.

## What FFP-401 Produces

A graduate who completes FFP-401 can:

Detect RF-emitting surveillance hardware in a space or vehicle using both commercial and self-built direction-finding tools. Understand the RF signatures that different categories of devices produce and interpret what detection results mean.

Conduct a structured TSCM sweep of a space, including wired-tap inspection, visual sweep, and RF characterization, using professional equipment.

Perform a hardware teardown and visual inspection for anomalous components.

Execute non-destructive entry fundamentals and understand how access to a space is gained and what evidence of prior access looks like.

Conduct a small-team safe-house selection and room-by-room clearing sequence.

The capstone exercise ties all of these together: entry, clearing, and a full TSCM sweep against planted devices under graded, scenario-based conditions.

FFP-401 is expected to open for scheduling Q4 2026. Prerequisites are FFP-201 and a Linux/CLI proficiency bar that can be satisfied through prior training, certification, or a technical screening at intake.
