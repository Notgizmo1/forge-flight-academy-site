Most military personnel know what a PACE plan is. Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency: a tiered communications structure that provides fallback options when the primary path is unavailable or compromised.

Fewer have practiced the fallback transitions under realistic conditions. When the primary path fails in training, operators often discover that the alternate they were counting on requires a setup step they did not complete, or a credential they stored in the primary communications platform, or a contact list that exists only in the compromised channel.

A PACE plan that has never been run is a document. The fallback capability is theoretical until the transition has been practiced.

## What a Live PACE Plan Reveals

Running a communications plan through actual use, including deliberate disruption of primary channels, surfaces two categories of failure that a paper plan does not.

**Setup failures.** The alternate and contingency channels require configuration, account creation, contact establishment, encryption key exchange, that most paper plans assume has been done but many operators have not completed. A plan that shows "Signal as alternate" is not an alternate until both parties have the app, have verified each other's identities, and have a shared understanding of when Signal replaces the primary channel.

**Behavioral failures.** Real personal information leaking into a cover identity is the most common behavioral failure. An operator running a masked communications identity who mentions something specific enough to link back to their real identity under questioning has broken the compartment. In a training environment, this failure is visible and can be corrected. In a live environment, it is not.

The only way to find these failures before they matter is to run the plan under conditions that reveal them.

## How FFP-201 Runs the Live Plan

[FFP-201](/courses/ffp-201/) starts the live PACE plan on Day 1 afternoon, not Day 5. Students configure and stand up their communications plan on Day 1, and the course itself runs through it for the remainder of the week.

The consequence is that by Day 3, most operators have already encountered and corrected failures they would not have discovered by reading a plan: a setup step they missed, an assumption about a contact that was not true, a behavioral pattern that leaked real information into a cover channel.

Grading is continuous. Inconsistencies like real personal information appearing in a channel where only cover identity is in use are graded as they happen, not reviewed only at the capstone. The capstone on Day 5 tests execution under disruption and elicitation pressure, but the capability has been built and corrected throughout the week.

## The Connection to Electronic Warfare

Communications security and electronic warfare literacy are addressing the same operating environment from different directions.

[FFR-201](/courses/ffr-201/) gives operators an understanding of the signal environment, what RF signatures look like, how devices are characterized through their emissions, and what spectrum awareness looks like in practice. FFP-201's communications security work addresses what is sent through that environment.

An operator who understands signal characterization and who is running an encrypted communications plan with correct compartmentation is significantly harder to target than one who has either skill in isolation. The two courses are designed to complement each other.

[FFP-401](/courses/ffp-401/), currently in development, takes this further into technical surveillance countermeasures, detecting the collection equipment itself, not just defending against what it collects.
